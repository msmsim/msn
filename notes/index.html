I read Darwin’s Dangerous Idea for the psychohistory book club. A couple things stood out.

First, it’s really easy to get nerdsniped by evolution and envision everything through an evolutionary lens. Check out Aneesh Sathe’s field guide to evolution and tell me that ain’t so.

Second, skyhooks versus cranes is a ridiculously useful frame for thinking about shared assumptions and things that are unknowable. There is always a crane, a mechanistic rationale behind the most wondrous phenomena. It’s just that, at a certain point, the cranes become too hard for us to distinguish and we resort to arguing about “deep truths”. Fortunately, these are not skyhooks but unresolved questions about cranes.

Third, the idea of using cranes to build more powerful cranes has altered my base perception of artificial intelligence. It’s softened my stance and led me to see it more as a form of normal technology. Yet it’s also caused me to pay more attention to the moral quandaries associated with lines that demarcate levels of consciousness.

Fourth—and perhaps most importantly, though—it’s caused me to reevaluate the role of intelligence.

Dennett talks about “universal acid”, a liquid that can eat through any container. Intelligence is pretty universal-acidish. Point an intelligence cannon at an arbitrary problem for long enough and that problem gets solved. The issue is that we humans are ourselves integral pieces of the many problems we’re trying to solve.

This may sound epochal, but it’s probably happened before at a smaller scale. The rise of the internet did the same thing with information, making it act like universal acid. There, we humans were also the impediment. And the bet was that with enough disembodied_information_, any problem could be solved. That didn’t turn out to be quite true, and we ended up evolving sophisticated sociocultural mechanisms for handling the abundant information flows of modern life.

A similar step will have to happen for intelligence. We’re in the unexpected abundance phase, and will soon enter the part of the story where we have to develop smart sociocultural mechanisms for dealing with vast levels of arbitrarily utilised intelligence. In the aggregate, those mechanisms will be smart. But per individual, those mechanisms will be extraordinarily dumb.

“Dumb” here is meant in the best sense of the word. Something that is self-evident and obvious to the agent with minimal deep reflective thought and contemplative action, and thus highly likely to be of maximal local value.

This is why Dennett says that Darwin’s idea is so dangerous. Darwin posited that for populations, it is always crunch time. Variations within that population yield advantages, and those advantages are amplified across generations. This dumb, banal algorithmic process called natural selection underpins life as we know it.

And the dumb use of smarts is, I believe, the most intelligent thing we as a species will ever actually do.

Winning finite games used to mean enhanced status for the winner. That status drove audience creation and brand power.

Now, the winning of finite games is too sparse an event to satisfy the demand for audiences to coalesce and for brands to attach to.

So we decompose the finite game into smaller and smaller increments, manufacturing events out of what used to be the process.

A world championship happens once a year. Too slow. So we decompose down to a season. Too slow. Down to a race, a match. Still too slow. Down to the buildup, the training, to the planning of the preparation. To the thinking and the talking about the planning of the preparation.

It isn’t the familiar “process over outcome,” “the score takes care of itself” thinking. That treats process as something separate, as sacred in and of itself.

This is the opposite. This is the evisceration of process itself, in favour of infinite sequences of nano finite games, each one framed to have a winner, with each competitor competing for drops of status in a barren landscape.

A training session isn’t won. A rehearsal isn’t won. A draft isn’t won. But legibility abhors a true process, so a winner gets imposed and the audience can continue to not really pay attention.

There’s an idea in philosophy that one already is the sort of person one aspires to be. This realisation, that one can simply decide to change, can occur in a moment.

Counter-positioned against this simple idea is a gargantuan array of self-improvement frameworks, behaviour modification approaches, conspicuously consumable goods and services nominally designed for these ends, and black books of hacks used to manipulate perception and action. And don’t forget the bewildering field of people and orgs pushing any and all of the above with varying efficacy.

This means that an individual changing to a considerable degree isn’t really that special. Live even just a little and such meaningful change is inevitable. But what about a pack, a team, a group, an organisation, a culture? Can a complete and effective re-orientation take place in a single moment across collectives? A whole bunch of overlapping industries and cabals would reply with a horrified, “No.” They’re wrong.

Consider the generic form. An event happens. The low-n-order effects of that event impact a group to a great degree. The extent of the impact is great enough that the group must respond near-instantly with a major change or adaptation of behaviour. They do, meeting the event and the surrounding environment with admirable aplomb.

The event does three things for the group. It makes the need for change common knowledge, so no one person has to guess whether the others are on board. It makes the change legitimate, so adaptation reads as competence rather than betrayal of what came before. And it makes the old state irretrievable, so there’s no rut left to relapse into.

What the event doesn’t do is make any material change to the matter of a group. The group has roughly the same capacity (latent and realised) as before; it is merely that sufficient stimuli emerged and catalysed a strong response. Unconsciously, the group decided, in the span of a moment, to change.

Counter to common knowledge, a collective can move fast. Terrifyingly fast, sometimes. It’s just that doing so requires 1) common knowledge of 2) a legitimate response to deal with 3) a forced entry into a new world state. Reality will supply that to everyone eventually but it’s sensible to be proactive and provide it on purpose. And if you can’t bring such a thing to the collective? Take the collective to the thing itself.

What’s a World Machine? It “is a large-scale civilizational system — an interlocking set of institutions, technologies, practices, and assumptions — that organizes human life across centuries. Not a nation, not an empire, not an ideology, but something longer-lived and more fundamental than any of those: the deep infrastructure of a historical era.”

At any one time, there’s three World Machines in play, each spanning approx. 400 years and overlapping to cover a 1,000 year span: these are the emerging (Dawn), the dominant (Day) and the declining (Dusk) machines. The contributors have identified the current set:

  • The Modernity Machine (c. 1200–1600 Dawn, 1600–2000 Day, now Dusk) — the organizing system of the modern era, characterized by Progress as its animating drive. A “pull” system: it pulled humanity toward an imagined future of universal development, scientific mastery, and liberal order.
  • The Divergence Machine (c. 1600–2000 Dawn, 2000– Day) — currently the dominant emerging framework. Where Modernity pulled toward convergence, Divergence is a “push” system: it produces radically different outcomes depending on local conditions, values, and trajectories. There is no single destination.
  • The Liveness Machine (Dawn, future study) — the next machine, whose outlines are only beginning to be visible.

This is all good and fun and useful, but I have been wondering: what sort of person does a particular Dawn / Day / Dusk machine favour?

Given that World Machines theory’s premise is informed by the notion of psychohistory, I’m loathe to start proposing that particular individuals will “win” or “lose” at particular games in particular World Machines. There’s a need to be apersonal. What I’m more comfortable proposing, however, is that certain populations will “do better” at particular games in particular World Machines than others.

There’s two lenses for this. The first is a lock and key. Within each World Machines there exists a minority population that is especially adapted to the conditions; they are the ones most suited to unlock the latent value inherent in the World Machine.

The other is more evolutionary; it’s about replication over time as a proxy for harvesting unlocked value, and it is not the same as optimality. As an absurd example: imagine a minority celibate population is responsible for all technological progress in the world. Their rate of replication is naturally capped and likely to be dwarfed by all the other non-celibate populations that can “go forth and multiply” off the back of “progress” without having to worry about generating more of it.

Certain demographics of humanity will be uniquely suited to particular World Machines and shall thrive because of certain predispositions and traits; others will be less suited and will not. Given that the long term goal of World Machine theory is “to combine qualitative historiographic analysis with quantitative methods and AI modeling to generate long-term forecasts and speculations,”, there’ll need to be a way to answer the question: “As an individual, how does one align to those forecasts and speculations?”

Dawns imply there are things to be learned; Days imply there are problems to be solved; Dusks imply there are things to unlearn. How does a person actually do that?

  • Heat
  • Time

  • Solids: curate relevant materials and put them in a container

  • Heat: point intelligence at the container using questions, conjectures etc.
  • Time: continue application of intelligence until the broth is intense and nutritious

There’s generally three failure modes for a physical activity:

  • Technical: lacking skill
  • Physical: lacking fitness
  • Psychological: lacking flow

For a slower activity like climbing, these failure modes can be identified in the moments before a move. One can loop through these and spot the most likely failure.

It’s like a premortem but happens within the context of the activity, not in a carved out spacetime beforehand. It’s obviously not like post-event learning, where you fail then update.
